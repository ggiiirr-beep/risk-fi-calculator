Data and simulation method

Historical U.S. markets

The bundled dataset contains 98 annual observations from 1928 to 2025: U.S. stock returns including dividends, 10-year U.S. Treasury bond total returns, and the same year’s CPI inflation. Source: Aswath Damodaran, NYU Stern: historical returns workbook, stocks and inflation retrieved October 8, 2026; bonds verified October 21, 2026. Earlier years include the predecessor history of the S&P 500.

How historical periods become simulations

By default, we sample five consecutive years at a time, then join independently selected blocks into a simulated future. For example, one block could contain 1973 to 1977, followed by a different historical block. Blocks can repeat. Stock returns, bond returns and inflation remain paired.

You can choose individual years or 3-, 5-, or 10-year blocks. Longer blocks preserve longer runs of historical conditions, but provide fewer distinct starting periods. Blocks never wrap from 2025 to 1928. This is resampled history, not one uninterrupted historical retirement.

What the probabilities mean

Both strategies face the same simulated markets. Separate forward simulations estimate checkpoint success assuming current withdrawals continue without later adjustments. The reported strategy success rate measures what actually happened in simulations with the guardrail adjustments.

For Risk FI, success means funding withdrawals and holding the FI target at the deadline. For Traditional FI, it means funding withdrawals through retirement and meeting the selected ending balance.

Other return models

Your plan calculators use your entered constant return and inflation. Custom-volatility simulations generate returns from your return and volatility assumptions; they do not replay historical periods. Historical mode uses the sampled returns and inflation, rather than your entered averages. Cash always uses your entered cash yield.

Assets and future changes

Basic projections grow each asset at its entered return. Contributions and withdrawals are shared proportionally across noncash assets. Historical guardrails rebalance stocks and bonds annually to their starting proportions. Custom-volatility mode models the combined investment portfolio using its weighted expected return and one volatility assumption. Cash uses its weighted entered yield.

Future income offsets planned withdrawals; surplus goes to cash. One-time additions go to investments, and expenses draw from assets under the selected cash policy. Inflation-adjusted entries preserve today’s purchasing power; fixed future-dollar entries lose purchasing power with inflation. These events also enter forward guardrail probability estimates, without assuming later guardrail adjustments. The FI target is unchanged.

Guardrails use annual, end-of-year cash flows; basic accumulation calculators use monthly cash flows. With future events, checkpoint probabilities use direct independent forward simulations, so runs can take several minutes. A temporary zero balance is recorded as depletion; if future income funds every withdrawal and the ending goal is met, the scheduled plan can still succeed.

How cash is used

Cash is part of total assets and earns its own return. Traditional FI accumulation keeps it separate; future income surpluses can add to it. RiskFi and GapFi apply their own cash-use choices without automatically refilling cash. Their downturn choice uses negative inflation-adjusted returns; a constant-return projection cannot model changing market conditions.

Guardrails use cash first in nonpositive combined investment-return years, then investments if needed. Positive years use investments first; remaining cash can cover a shortage. After positive years, investments can be transferred into cash toward the reserve target. Transfers do not create assets.

Limits and disclaimer

These are U.S. stock and 10-year Treasury scenarios, not global markets or all bond types. Historical blocks cannot capture every future market regime. Finite simulation samples and probability approximations introduce uncertainty; an estimated 100% is not certainty.

For educational use, not personalized financial advice. Projections and estimated probabilities are hypothetical, depend on assumptions, and do not guarantee future results. Historical returns may not repeat. Taxes and fees are not separately modeled; account for them in your inputs.
